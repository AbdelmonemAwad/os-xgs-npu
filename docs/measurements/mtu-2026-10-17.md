# The MTU, from 576 to 9000, on the three-appliance bench

Measured 2026-10-17 on [the bench](bench-2026-10-17.md): one TCP stream for ten seconds in each
direction, in clear text and through the IPsec tunnels (AES-GCM-256, in the engines at both ends),
at eight MTUs from the smallest to the largest. Every run's window was photographed when it
finished; the 52 pictures are in [`docs/images/mtu/`](../images/mtu/) and below each table.

![The bench](../images/bench/three-appliances-2026-10-17.jpg)

## How

- **The MTU was set on both ends of each far link** through OPNsense - the `<mtu>` of the interface's
  entry, applied with OPNsense's own reconfigure, the way the GUI's interface page does it - and read
  back from the interface: the 10G fibre between the two XGS 3300s (module port A1 and F1), and the
  gigabit link from the second XGS 3300's Port8 to the XGS 136's Port8.
- **The far link is the narrowest hop.** Above 1500 the PC's own 10G link runs at 9000 at both ends
  (jumbo packets 9014 on the PC's NIC), so the far link's MTU decides the segment size; the TCP
  maximum segment size each connection actually got is printed in every window.
- **The gigabit link ~~stops at 2000~~ was opened to 9000.** Port8 is behind the switch chip on both
  appliances, which carried frames of at most 2048 bytes
  ([the MTU is the LIF's](../findings/the-mtu-is-the-lifs.md)), ~~so the XGS 136 was measured up to
  2000 and the fibre up to 9000~~ - so the XGS 136 was first measured up to 2000. Then Port Control 2
  of port 8 was written from `0x1480` (2,048 bytes) to `0x2480` (10,240) on both switch chips, with
  `mvsw` on each coprocessor, and the 136 was measured at 4000, 6000 and 9000 as well - after the fix
  for what that found, [issue 422](https://github.com/AbdelmonemAwad/os-xgs-npu/issues/422). Written
  by hand for these measurements; since the same night a start hook writes all ten ports of both
  chips at every boot (`scripts/xgs/switch-jumbo.sh`).
- **What each far host had learned was forgotten** at every step (`net.inet.tcp.hostcache.purgenow`),
  and the PC's own path-MTU cache before every run.
- **Two clients on the PC.** The direction in which the PC sends was measured with iperf3 3.20 under
  WSL; the direction in which it receives with iperf3 3.22 for Windows - see
  [what went wrong first](#what-went-wrong-first).

## What the tables say

- **Into a 3300's host the rate follows the frame**: from 757 Mbit/s at 576 to 7.11 Gbit/s at 9000
  in clear text - 9.4 times the rate for 17 times the segment - and the tunnel keeps up all the way:
  7.41 Gbit/s at 9000, with the engines encrypting on the second 3300 and decrypting on the first.
- **The tunnel costs most at the smallest frame**: 531 against 757 Mbit/s at 576, where ESP's
  envelope is the largest share of every packet. From 1000 upwards, towards the 3300, IPsec is within
  ten per cent of clear text and at 1000, 1280 and 9000 above it. In the other direction it is faster
  than clear text from 2000 on - 3.85 against 3.16 Gbit/s at 2000, 4.46 against 3.55 at 9000 - not yet
  explained.
- **Out of the first 3300's host the curve turns over above 4000**: 4.83 and 5.56 Gbit/s at 4000,
  then 3.55 and 4.46 at 9000. ~~Not yet taken apart: the sender here is that appliance's host, and the
  receiver is a different client from the other direction.~~ **Taken apart: the PC drops them.** Every
  segment the sender retransmitted is one the PC's NIC counted as discarded on arrival, to the
  segment - 864 and 864 at 1500, 1717 and 1717 at 4000, 1322 and 1328 at 9000 - and with four streams
  the same path carries 9.91 Gbit/s of 9000-byte frames with 79 discards. One stream lands on one
  receive queue of the PC's X722, which has 512 receive buffers; the appliances drop nothing. The
  same holds through the tunnel, so its apparent advantage in this direction is the noise of those
  drops. The window it was read in:

  ![sender retransmits against the PC NIC's discards, one stream at each MTU, and four streams at 9000](../images/mtu/drops-old3300-to-pc.png)
- **The gigabit link is full from 1000 on**: 908 to 961 Mbit/s in clear text, the line rate less the
  headers; IPsec is 2 to 8 per cent below it ~~.~~ up to 2000, and within 1 to 3 per cent from 4000,
  where 989 and 991 Mbit/s at 9000 are the line rate. At 576 the 136 sends 867 Mbit/s and takes 726,
  and the tunnel at 576 costs it the most of any row - 411 Mbit/s in.

## What went wrong first

Two things in the measuring rather than in the appliances, and both are why the tables above were
measured the way the [How](#how) section says - and a third in the XGS 136's driver.

1. **The PC kept a path MTU it had learned.** The first pass from 1000 to 1500 ran with a 500-byte
   segment at every step: Linux holds a learned path MTU for ten minutes, the one learned through the
   tunnel at 576 was still held, and the flush in the script ran as a user that cannot flush it.
   Those runs were thrown away and measured again with the cache flushed as root before every run.
2. **WSL drops a received frame above 1500 bytes.** Every run in which the PC received, above 1500,
   measured 0.00 bits/sec, through the tunnel and without it. A 9000-byte ping from Windows to the
   second 3300 was answered; the same ping from WSL was not, from 1600 bytes up, with WSL's MTU at
   9000 - its mirrored networking passes large frames out and not in. Between the two XGS 3300s over
   the fibre, 9000-byte frames ran at 8.87 Gbit/s one way and 8.19 the other. So every run in which
   the PC receives, at every MTU, was measured again with iperf3 for Windows.
3. **The XGS 136 stopped receiving at the first frame above 4096 bytes** -
   [issue 422](https://github.com/AbdelmonemAwad/os-xgs-npu/issues/422). With both switch chips
   carrying jumbo frames, DF pings of 1472, 2000 and 4000 bytes answered; one of 8000 did not, and
   from then on no TCP connection to the 136 worked, on any port. Its console showed why: receive
   queue 0 stopped at one descriptor (`rx 200/56`) and `rx_nobuf` climbing a thousand a second. The
   driver took the frame's mbuf with `m_get2`, which serves at most a page and answers NULL for
   anything larger, every time - and a failure there leaves the frame for the next tick. The same
   call built the tunnel's ESP envelopes, so above 4096 bytes not one segment left the 136 encrypted
   (6000 and 9000: 0.00 bits/sec, `tx_nobuf` counting). Both now take a cluster of the frame's size.
